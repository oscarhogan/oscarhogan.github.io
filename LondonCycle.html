Cycleway Planning

Multi-Criteria Evaluation and Network Analysis

Using GIS to inform more sustainable, equitable active travel infrastucture. How can data-driven insights help government agencies reach their policy aspirations?

How can London become Europe's greatest large cycling city?

The benefits of cycling are well established. Both on the individual; Disease risk reduction and higher life expectancy - as well as on society; reduced traffic fatilites and lower ambient air pollution. Many European cities use segregated cycleway infrastructure to improve the perception of safety around cycling, which is the biggest impedance to increasing uptake.

While London has made strides in creating a less hostile cycling environment, studies mark a divide between those that choose to cycle and those that do not. Demographic differences in tendency to cycle reflect an environment where cycleway planning is often built upon existing usage patterns.

How then, does the consideration of a range of needs and desires in the cycleway planning process inform network planning? And how can geospatial data analysis be used to help create an active travel environment that best reflects the needs of London's diverse population, achieving the cities aspiration of becoming a leading cycle city.

Multi-Criteria Evaluation

Understanding where cycleway investment is most prudent neccessitates an understanding of how and why people cycle, and the barriers they face. While not all-encompassing, Multi-Criteria Evaluation (MCE) provides a framework to consider the role of different factors.

MCE weighs different input criteria on the basis of importance, assigning geographic areas a total value. In the case of cycleway planning, looking for locations which best satisify the demands of a diverse potential userbase, informs an inclusive network.

An adjusted version of the Bicycle Levels of Service (BLoS) which is used to assess the quality of existing infrastructure was developed to reflect the needs of user groups, underepresented among cyclists who may hold differing route priorities. For example older adults may value routes with lower maximum gradients whereas female cyclists may prioritise street lighting and visibility.

Network Analysis

Pairing MCE with Network Analysis; a geospatial method which evaluates the most favourable path between an origin-destination point pair using a network dataset allows for the identification of corridors which could best promote inclusive cycling.

This involves attaching MCE attributes to the network dataset based on proximity, before being used as impedances, reflecting the impact of different factors the traversing cost of a path segment. For example, the difference in elevation between points at the start and end of a road are taken as the gradient value, which if exceeding a certain threshold adds traversal cost.
