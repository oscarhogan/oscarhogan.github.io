How can London become Europe's greatest large cycling city?

The benefits of cycling are well established. Both on the individual; Disease risk reduction and higher life expectancy - as well as on society; reduced traffic fatilites and lower ambient air pollution. Many European cities use segregated cycleway infrastructure to improve the perception of safety around cycling, which is the biggest impedance to increasing uptake.

While London has made strides in creating a less hostile cycling environment, studies mark a divide between those that choose to cycle and those that do not. Demographic differences in tendency to cycle reflect an environment where cycleway planning is often built upon existing usage patterns.

How then, does the consideration of a range of needs and desires in the cycleway planning process inform network planning? And how can geospatial data analysis be used to help create an active travel environment that best reflects the needs of London's diverse population, achieving the cities aspiration of becoming a leading cycle city.

Multi-Criteria Evaluation

Understanding where cycleway investment is beneficial neccessitates an understanding of how and why people cycle, and the barriers they face. While not all-encompassing, Multi-Criteria Evaluation (MCE) provides a framework to consider the role of different factors.

MCE weighs different input criteria on the basis of importance, assigning geographic areas a total value. In the case of cycleway planning, looking for locations which best satisify the demands of a diverse userbase, informs an inclusive network.

Developing a case specific MCE using inclusivity orientated weighting and factors

To inform route selection reflecting the needs of South Londons dievrse population, a range of environmental and demographic impedence factors were compiled into a modified BLOS informed MCE. Each factor was included and weighted based on current literature on progressive active travel planning, focusing on underepresented user groups.

Literature raised several route factors, as disprotionality impacting underpresented groups like elderly, female and ethnic-minority cyclists. These include Street Lighting, Gradient, Intersection traversal, service access and cycle parking availability. These are in addition to a number of routeing metrics routinely used by transport planners to to inform network allignment.

Betweeness Centrality

How central a road segment is within the network. Indicates its importance in connecting different parts of the wider area. Segments with a higher betweeness centrality are given lower routing cost, as they facilitate a greater range of journeys.

Cycleway Contiguity

How integral a road segment is to the existing cycleway network. If a segment is already part of the network a scaled cost reduction is calculated based on how large the adjacent network is.

Basket of Services

Proximity to services like healthcare, education, employment and recreation, integral to the needs of a diverse userbase. Greater proximity, informs a higher score, highlighting areas where cycleway investment could best facilitate activie travel across a range of trip types.

Network Analysis

Network Analysis is a geospatial method which evaluates the most favourable path between an origin-destination point pair. Pairing this with an MCE with allows the identification ofcorridors which could best promote inclusive cycling.

This is done by attaching MCE attributes to the network dataset based on proximity, using them as impedances, reflecting the impact of different factors on the 'cost' of traversing a path segment. For example, the difference in elevation between points at the start and end of a road are taken as the gradient value, which if exceeding a certain threshold is a barrier to cycling, increasing traversal cost.

The route with the lowest accumulated cost between an origin and point pair is taken as the most favourable route.

High Priority Allignments

Viable cycleway allignments were identified using custom Python route-finding algorithm which uses the cumulative route cost to calculate the best route between a set of 5000 point-origin pairs.

Road sections raised in at least 1% of instances were considered viable, and those above 2% highly suitable, returning a set of locations where cycleway infrastructure should be prioritised.
